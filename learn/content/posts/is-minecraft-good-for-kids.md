Children can talk about Minecraft for hours, and it can be hard to tell whether all that screen time is doing them any good. The honest answer is that it depends on how they play. Building a working castle is very different from watching other people play on YouTube all afternoon.

This guide explains what Minecraft is, what children can really learn from it, how to keep them safe online, and which version to buy.

> **The short answer:** Minecraft is one of the better games a child can play, especially in single-player or with friends you know. The main risks come from public multiplayer servers, and those are easy to avoid.

## What is Minecraft?

Minecraft is a game about building with blocks in a huge open world. There are two main ways to play:

- **Creative mode:** unlimited blocks and no danger. It is a digital building set, and a good place to start with younger children.
- **Survival mode:** players gather materials, craft tools and survive monsters at night. It has a bit of challenge, and some cartoon-style fighting.

There is no set story and no required ending. Your child decides what to do, which is a big part of why it stays interesting for years.

## What can children learn from Minecraft?

It would be easy to overstate this. Playing Minecraft will not teach your child to code by itself. But some kinds of play do build real skills:

- **Planning and problem solving:** working out how to build something before starting.
- **Maths and space:** counting blocks, using coordinates and thinking in 3D.
- **Logic:** [redstone circuits](/minecraft-redstone-for-beginners/) use the same logic gates as real computers.
- **Coding basics:** [commands](/minecraft-commands-for-beginners/) teach exact instructions and fixing errors.
- **Teamwork:** building something big with a friend or brother or sister.
- **Patience:** big projects take days, and things go wrong along the way.

The most useful question to ask your child is not "what did you play?" but "what did you make?"

## Is Minecraft safe?

The game itself is mild. The risks mostly appear when children play online with people they do not know.

- **Public servers:** these are run by other people and companies, and each has its own rules, moderators and quality. Children can meet strangers and see bad language there.
- **Chat:** players can message each other on multiplayer servers.
- **Spending:** the in-game Marketplace sells add-ons, and some servers sell upgrades. These can cost real money.
- **Fake downloads:** "free mods" from unknown websites can carry viruses.
- **Time:** because there is no ending, it can be hard to stop. Agree a limit in advance, and use a timer.

## Setting up a safe account

Minecraft uses a Microsoft account. If your child has a child account linked to your own adult account, you control the settings. Age rules vary between countries, and this is how Minecraft describes them:

- Multiplayer is **turned off by default** on child accounts in both Java and Bedrock Edition.
- You manage settings at **account.xbox.com/settings**, signed in as the adult.
- Under **Online Safety**, you can choose whether your child can join multiplayer games, join clubs and add friends.
- Under **Privacy**, you can choose who can contact your child: everyone, friends only, or nobody.

These settings can take a little time to apply, so do not panic if a change does not show straight away. Sign out and restart the game.

## The safest ways to play together

Children often want to play with friends, and that is one of the best parts of the game. From safest to riskiest:

1. **Single-player:** no one else involved.
2. **At home:** playing with family over your own Wi-Fi.
3. **A private Realm:** a small private world, run through a paid subscription, where your child invites people they know. Minecraft says a Realm can hold your child plus up to 10 friends.
4. **Public servers:** the most open option. If your child is old enough, look at the server together first, and turn chat off if you can.

## Java or Bedrock: which should you buy?

There are two main versions of Minecraft. They look similar but they are different under the hood.

| | **Bedrock Edition** | **Java Edition** |
|---|---|---|
| Works on | Windows, consoles, phones and tablets | Windows, Mac and Linux computers |
| Plays with | Friends on other devices | Other Java players |
| Good for | Most families and younger children | Older children interested in mods |

On a Windows PC, the two editions are usually sold together, but check what is included before you buy. If your child has friends on consoles or phones, Bedrock is normally the one to choose.

## What about Minecraft Education?

Minecraft Education is a separate version designed for schools. It comes with lessons and a built-in **Code Builder**, where children can program the game with blocks or text using Microsoft MakeCode.

This matters if coding is your main goal, because the older way of linking MakeCode to the normal game stopped being supported in December 2023.

Before you buy, here is what to expect:

- Families can buy it, but the cost for people outside eligible schools is **36 US dollars per user, per year**, as listed by Minecraft at the time of writing.
- You buy it through a free Microsoft 365 admin account, so it takes a little more work than buying a normal game.
- If your child's school already uses it, they may be able to log in at home for free. Ask the school first.

For many families, it is not necessary. Commands and redstone are already in the normal game and cost nothing extra, and [Roblox Studio](/how-to-make-a-roblox-game-for-beginners/) is a free way to learn text coding.

## Helping your child get more out of it

- **Set a project:** "Can you build our house?" works much better than "go and play".
- **Ask for a tour:** children love showing their builds, and you will learn a lot.
- **Try commands together:** our [commands guide](/minecraft-commands-for-beginners/) is a fun 20 minutes.
- **Have a plan for time:** agree when screens go off before the game starts.
- **Move on to making:** when a child wants to know how games work, they are ready to try coding.

## Frequently asked questions

**What age is Minecraft suitable for?** Many children start at around 6 to 8 in Creative mode, with a parent nearby. Age ratings differ by region, so check the rating for your country.

**Is Minecraft violent?** Survival mode includes fighting monsters, but it is blocky and cartoon-like. Peaceful difficulty removes hostile monsters, and Creative mode has no danger.

**Does Minecraft teach coding?** Not on its own. Commands, redstone and Code Builder introduce coding ideas, and many children then move on to [Scratch](/scratch-project-ideas-for-beginners/), [Python](/python-projects-for-beginners/) or [Roblox](/roblox-scripting-for-beginners/).

**Can my child play without being online?** Yes. Single-player worlds work without internet, once the game is installed and set up.

**Are mods safe?** Only download mods from sources you trust, and do it together. Bedrock's in-game Marketplace is safer than random websites, but it costs money.

**Where can I find the official guidance?** Minecraft publishes a [parents' guide](https://www.minecraft.net/en-us/article/parents--guide-minecraft) and a page on [parental controls](https://www.minecraft.net/en-us/article/parental-controls). Settings can change, so check there for the latest details.
