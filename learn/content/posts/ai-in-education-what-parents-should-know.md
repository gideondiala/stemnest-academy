A few years ago, "AI in schools" sounded like science fiction. Today, many children use AI tools for homework help, practice quizzes and explanations, often before their parents and teachers have worked out the rules. If you're unsure what this means for your child, you're not alone.

This guide gives you a balanced picture: how AI is being used in education, what the real benefits are, what to watch out for, and practical steps you can take at home.

## How AI is showing up in children's learning

AI in education isn't one thing. It appears in several different forms:

- **AI chatbots and assistants** that answer questions, explain topics and help with writing.
- **Adaptive learning apps** that adjust questions to a child's level, giving easier or harder tasks depending on their answers.
- **Practice and feedback tools** that mark answers instantly and suggest what to work on next.
- **Accessibility tools** such as text-to-speech, speech-to-text and translation, which can make learning easier for children with additional needs or those learning in a second language.
- **Teacher tools** that help with planning lessons, creating quizzes and giving feedback.

Some of these are built into apps children already use. Others are general-purpose chatbots that weren't designed with children in mind.

## The real benefits

Used well, AI can genuinely help children learn:

- **Patient, on-demand explanations.** A child stuck on long division at 8pm can get a step-by-step explanation in a different way from the textbook.
- **Practice at the right level.** Adaptive tools can keep children challenged without overwhelming them.
- **Instant feedback.** Children can find out straight away whether they're on the right track, instead of waiting days for marking.
- **Confidence to ask "silly" questions.** Some children are more willing to ask a computer something they'd be embarrassed to ask in class.
- **Support for different learners.** Reading aids, translation and speech tools can open up learning for many children.

## The risks worth knowing about

AI also brings real challenges that parents should understand.

### AI can be confidently wrong

AI chatbots sometimes produce answers that sound convincing but are incorrect, or even make up facts and sources. Children may not have the knowledge to spot these mistakes. Our explainer on [how AI chatbots work](/how-do-ai-chatbots-work-explained/) explains why this happens.

### Shortcuts instead of learning

If AI writes an essay or solves maths problems for a child, the homework gets done but the learning doesn't happen. Over time, this can leave gaps in understanding that show up in exams, where AI isn't available.

### Privacy and data

Many AI services collect what users type. Children may share personal information without realising it. Many general chatbots also have minimum age requirements, often 13, with parental permission needed for under-18s on some services.

### Over-reliance

Thinking hard, getting stuck and working through problems is how the brain learns. If children reach for AI at the first sign of difficulty, they miss that valuable struggle.

### Fairness and bias

AI learns from data, and that data can contain biases. Children benefit from understanding that AI reflects the information it was trained on, which isn't always fair or complete.

## What schools are doing

Schools and education authorities around the world are working out how to respond. Approaches vary widely: some restrict AI for homework and assessments, others teach students how to use it responsibly, and many are developing guidance on what counts as acceptable use. Policies are changing quickly, so the most useful step is simple: **ask your child's school what its current rules are**, and make sure your child knows them too.

## How to help your child use AI well

### 1. Talk about it openly

Ask your child whether they use AI tools, which ones, and what for. Curiosity works better than suspicion. You might be surprised how thoughtfully many children already use them.

### 2. Agree on "tutor, not ghostwriter"

A simple rule many families find helpful: **AI can help you understand, but it can't do the work for you.**

Good uses:
- "Explain fractions in a different way."
- "Give me five practice questions on the water cycle."
- "Why doesn't my code work?"
- "Check whether my paragraph makes sense."

Not okay:
- "Write my essay about the Second World War."
- "Answer these homework questions."

### 3. Teach the habit of checking

Encourage your child to treat AI answers as a starting point, not the final word. Can they find the same information in their textbook or a trusted website? Does the answer actually make sense?

### 4. Protect personal information

Make sure your child knows never to share their full name, school, address, phone number, passwords or photos with AI tools or chatbots.

### 5. Check age limits and settings

Read the age requirements for any AI tool your child wants to use, and look for parental controls or child-friendly versions.

### 6. Keep the thinking human

Praise effort, persistence and original ideas. When your child is stuck, try asking questions ("What do you already know? What have you tried?") before reaching for AI.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I let my child use AI for homework?** It depends on the school's rules and how it's used. Using AI to understand a topic or practise can help learning. Using it to complete work for them usually doesn't.

**At what age can children use AI chatbots?** Many general chatbots require users to be at least 13, and some need parental consent for under-18s. Always check the specific service's terms.

**Will AI replace teachers?** AI can support learning, but teachers do far more than deliver information. They motivate, notice when a child is struggling, build relationships and teach social and emotional skills. Most experts see AI as a tool for teachers, not a replacement.

**How can I tell if my child is relying on AI too much?** Watch for work that doesn't sound like them, difficulty explaining their own answers, or frustration when they can't use AI. A gentle conversation and clear family rules usually help.
