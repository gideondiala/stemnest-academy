"AI can write code now. Is there any point in my child learning to program?"

It is one of the most common questions parents ask us, and a fair one. AI assistants can produce working code from a simple request, and AI is changing how many jobs are done. So should children skip coding and "learn AI" instead?

**The short answer: it isn't either-or.** The children who will do best are those who understand **how computers think** (coding) *and* **how AI works and how to use it well** (AI literacy). Coding is the foundation that makes the AI part make sense. This guide explains why, and gives you a practical plan by age.

## What does "learning AI" actually mean?

"Learning AI" can mean three very different things, and it helps to separate them:

| Level | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Using AI tools** | Asking an AI assistant to explain, summarise or create something | Asking a chatbot to explain fractions in a different way |
| **2. Understanding AI** | Knowing how AI learns from data, why it makes mistakes, and where bias comes from | Training a small model and seeing it fail on new examples |
| **3. Building with AI** | Collecting data, training models, and putting AI inside apps and games | Making a game that responds to hand gestures from a webcam |

Level 1 is easy to pick up and changes every few months as tools change. Levels 2 and 3 are the lasting skills — and they rely heavily on the thinking skills children build through coding.

## Why coding still matters (even when AI can write code)

### 1. Coding teaches a way of thinking, not just a language

When children code, they practise breaking a big problem into small steps, spotting patterns, thinking logically, and testing their ideas. These skills — often called **computational thinking** — are useful whether or not they become programmers. They are also exactly the skills needed to give an AI clear instructions and judge what it produces.

### 2. You can't check what you don't understand

AI tools are impressive but imperfect. They can produce code that looks right but has bugs, security problems or subtle mistakes, and they can state wrong facts confidently. A person who understands code can **read, test and fix** what the AI produces. A person who doesn't has to trust it blindly.

### 3. AI makes coders faster — it doesn't replace understanding

Professional developers increasingly use AI assistants to speed up routine work, much like a calculator speeds up arithmetic. But we still teach children arithmetic, because understanding numbers is what lets you use a calculator sensibly. The same is true of coding and AI.

### 4. Building things is motivating

There is real joy in a child making a game their friends can play, or a website about their favourite hobby. That sense of "I made this" builds confidence and persistence — and creating, rather than only consuming, technology is a great habit for life.

## Why children should learn about AI too

Coding alone is no longer the whole picture. Children are growing up surrounded by AI — in search, recommendations, photo filters, games and homework help. Understanding it helps them:

- **Use it wisely** — knowing when an AI answer might be wrong, and checking it.
- **Stay safe** — recognising AI-generated fake images, voices and messages.
- **Think about fairness** — understanding that AI learns from data, and biased data can lead to unfair results.
- **Get creative** — building their own small AI projects, which is now possible even for primary-age children with free tools.

## An age-by-age plan

Every child is different, so treat these as rough guides rather than rules.

### Ages 6–8: Play and logic

- **Coding:** block-based coding such as ScratchJr or Scratch — making characters move, tell stories and react to clicks.
- **AI:** simple, guided activities that show "the computer learns from examples", such as sorting pictures into groups.
- **Focus:** curiosity, sequencing and confidence.

### Ages 8–11: Build real projects

- **Coding:** Scratch games with scores, levels and variables. (Try our [catch game](/how-to-make-a-catch-game-in-scratch/) and [maze game](/how-to-make-a-maze-game-in-scratch/) tutorials.)
- **AI:** train a first model with a free tool like Teachable Machine and discover why it makes mistakes ([our step-by-step guide](/train-an-ai-model-with-teachable-machine/)).
- **Focus:** loops, conditions, variables, debugging, and the idea that AI learns from data.

### Ages 11–14: Move to text-based code

- **Coding:** Python and the web languages — HTML, CSS and JavaScript ([build your first website](/build-your-first-website-html-css/)).
- **AI:** use AI models inside their own programs; talk about bias, privacy and how to check AI answers.
- **Focus:** writing and reading real code, larger projects, and responsible use of AI assistants.

### Ages 14–17: Specialise and build a portfolio

- **Coding:** deeper projects in an area they love — apps, games, websites or data science.
- **AI:** machine learning with Python, working with datasets, and using AI assistants as a tool while still understanding every line they submit.
- **Focus:** independence, real-world projects, and a portfolio that shows what they can do.

## Using AI tools safely at home

- **Check the age rules.** Many AI chatbots require users to be at least 13, and some require parental permission until 18. Check each service's terms before your child signs up.
- **Use it as a tutor, not a shortcut.** Encourage prompts like *"Explain why my code doesn't work"* rather than *"Do my homework"*.
- **Verify important facts** with a trusted source.
- **Protect personal information.** Children should never share their full name, school, address or photos with AI tools.
- **Talk about it.** Ask your child to show you what they made and how they used AI to help.

## How to choose a good coding or AI course

Look for:

- ✅ **Projects, not just videos** — children should build things every lesson.
- ✅ **A clear progression** from beginner to advanced, so skills build on each other.
- ✅ **Live help** when they get stuck — this is where most beginners give up.
- ✅ **AI taught responsibly**, including how it works and its limits, not just which buttons to press.

Be cautious of:

- ⚠️ Promises that a child will "become an AI expert" in a few weeks.
- ⚠️ Courses that are only about using one specific app.
- ⚠️ No way to see what your child has actually built.

## The bottom line

In 2026, the question isn't *coding or AI* — it's how to give children a strong foundation that lets them understand, use and build with both. Start with coding to build thinking skills and confidence, bring in AI concepts early so children understand the technology around them, and keep the focus on **creating** rather than just consuming.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will AI replace programmers?** AI is changing how programming is done, and it automates some routine tasks. But building good software still needs people who understand problems, make design decisions, check quality and take responsibility for the result. Understanding code is becoming *more* valuable for working well alongside AI, not less.

**What is the best age to start coding?** Many children start block-based coding at 6 to 8 and move to text-based languages like Python around 10 to 12. Starting later is absolutely fine — teenagers often progress very quickly.

**Should my child learn Python or JavaScript first?** Python is often recommended for its simple, readable syntax and its use in AI and data science. JavaScript is the language of web pages and is great for children who want to build websites and browser games. Either is a good first text language.

**Can young children really learn about AI?** Yes. With free, visual tools, children as young as 8 can train simple models and understand the key idea: AI learns from examples, so the examples matter.
