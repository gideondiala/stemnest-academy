Most students revise the same way: they read their notes over and over, highlight a few lines in bright yellow, and hope it sticks. It feels productive. Unfortunately, it's one of the least effective ways to learn.

The good news is that scientists who study memory have found techniques that work much better, and they're not difficult. Here are ten revision tips that make a real difference, whether you're preparing for a class test, end-of-year exams or major exams like GCSEs or WAEC.

## 1. Test yourself instead of rereading (active recall)

This is the single most powerful revision technique there is. **Active recall** means pulling information *out* of your memory rather than putting it in again.

Instead of reading your notes on photosynthesis, close the book and ask yourself: *"What is photosynthesis? What does a plant need for it? What does it produce?"* Then check.

Why it works: every time you struggle to remember something and succeed, your brain strengthens that memory. Rereading feels easier, but easy isn't the goal. A little bit of struggle is a sign you're learning.

**Ways to practise active recall:**

- Answer past papers and practice questions.
- Cover your notes and write down everything you remember, then compare.
- Use flashcards: a question on the front, the answer on the back.
- Explain a topic out loud as if you're teaching it.

## 2. Space it out (spaced repetition)

Cramming everything into the night before might get you through a test, but most of it is forgotten within days. **Spaced repetition** means revisiting a topic several times, with gaps in between.

A simple pattern:

- Learn a topic today.
- Test yourself on it **tomorrow**.
- Again after **three days**.
- Again after **a week**.
- Again after **two weeks**.

Each time, you'll remember a bit more and need less time to refresh it. This is why starting revision early matters so much.

## 3. Mix up your subjects (interleaving)

It's tempting to spend a whole afternoon on one topic. Research suggests that **mixing related topics or question types** in one session, called interleaving, often leads to better long-term learning, especially in maths and science. It forces your brain to decide which method fits each question, which is exactly what you have to do in an exam.

For example, instead of doing 20 fraction questions in a row, mix fractions, percentages and ratios.

## 4. Make a realistic revision timetable

A plan stops you from panicking and spending all your time on the subjects you already like.

1. **List every subject and topic** you need to revise.
2. **Mark each one** green (confident), amber (okay) or red (worried).
3. **Count the days** until your exams.
4. **Spread the topics out**, giving more sessions to red topics and coming back to every topic more than once.
5. **Include rest days and fun.** A timetable you can't stick to is worse than none.

## 5. Study in focused bursts

Long sessions without breaks don't work well. Try the **Pomodoro Technique**: 25 minutes of focused work, then a 5-minute break. Our guide to the [Pomodoro Technique for students](/pomodoro-technique-for-students/) explains how to adapt it for your age, and even shows how to build your own timer.

## 6. Turn notes into something new

Copying notes word for word isn't revision. Transform them instead:

- **Mind maps:** put the topic in the middle and branch out with key ideas, using colours and small drawings.
- **Summary cards:** squeeze a whole topic onto one index card. Deciding what to keep forces you to understand it.
- **Diagrams and timelines:** brilliant for science processes and history.
- **Teach it:** explain the topic to a family member, a pet, or an empty chair.

## 7. Practise under exam conditions

Do at least a few past papers **timed, without notes, in a quiet room**. This helps you:

- get used to the timing, so you don't run out of time,
- learn the style of questions and what examiners want,
- find gaps in your knowledge while there's still time to fix them.

Afterwards, mark your answers and spend time on what you got wrong. That's where the biggest improvements come from.

## 8. Put your phone away

Even having your phone on the desk, face down, can pull your attention away. During revision sessions, put it in another room or switch on a focus mode. Save messages and social media for your breaks.

## 9. Look after your brain

Your brain is part of your body, and it learns best when you look after yourself:

- **Sleep:** your brain strengthens new memories while you sleep. All-nighters work against you.
- **Water and food:** stay hydrated and eat regular meals.
- **Movement:** a short walk or some exercise helps concentration and lowers stress.
- **Rest:** time with friends and family isn't wasted. It recharges you.

## 10. Be kind to yourself about nerves

Feeling nervous before exams is completely normal. A bit of stress can even help you focus. If worry starts to take over:

- Remember that you've prepared, and preparation reduces nerves.
- Try slow breathing: breathe in for four counts, hold for four, breathe out for six.
- Talk to someone you trust, like a parent, teacher or friend.

## A sample revision week

Here's what a balanced week might look like for a student with exams in a month:

| Day | After school |
|---|---|
| Monday | 2 pomodoros maths (practice questions), 1 pomodoro biology flashcards |
| Tuesday | 2 pomodoros English (plan essay answers), 1 pomodoro maths review |
| Wednesday | Rest day |
| Thursday | 2 pomodoros chemistry (diagrams), 1 pomodoro history timeline |
| Friday | 1 timed past paper section, then mark it |
| Saturday | 3 pomodoros on "red" topics, then free time |
| Sunday | Quick self-test on the week's topics, plan next week |

## For parents

You can help without becoming a drill sergeant. Help your child make a realistic timetable, create a quiet study space, offer to quiz them with flashcards, and make sure they're sleeping and eating well. Praise effort and good habits, not just marks.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the best way to revise?** Active recall (testing yourself) combined with spaced repetition (revisiting topics over time). These two techniques have strong support from learning research.

**How many hours a day should I revise?** It depends on your age and how close the exams are. Quality matters more than quantity: a few focused sessions with breaks beat long, distracted hours.

**Is it okay to revise the night before?** A light review the evening before is fine, but cramming new material late at night usually backfires. Sleep is more valuable.

**Do highlighting and rereading help?** They can help you get familiar with material, but on their own they're much less effective than testing yourself. Use them as a starting point, not your main method.
