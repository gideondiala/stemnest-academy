Many parents want their children to learn coding, then hit an obvious problem: *"I can't code myself. How am I supposed to teach it?"*

Here's the reassuring truth. You don't need to know how to code to help your child learn. What children need most from a parent isn't technical knowledge. It's encouragement, a bit of structure, and someone who's genuinely interested in what they make. This guide shows you how to provide all three.

## Why coding is worth learning

Coding isn't only for children who want to become software engineers. Learning to program builds skills that help in school and in life:

- **Problem solving.** Coding is all about breaking a big problem into small, manageable steps.
- **Logical thinking.** Children learn to think through cause and effect: if this happens, then that happens.
- **Resilience.** Programs rarely work first time. Children learn to treat mistakes as puzzles to solve rather than failures, which is a lesson that transfers to everything.
- **Creativity.** Coding lets children make games, animations, stories and websites about the things they love.
- **Maths confidence.** Coordinates, angles, variables and patterns all appear naturally in coding projects, often making maths feel more useful and less abstract.
- **Understanding the world around them.** Technology shapes daily life. Knowing how it works helps children become creators and thoughtful users, not just consumers.

And yes, digital skills are increasingly valuable in almost every career, from medicine to design to business.

## What's the best age to start?

There's no single perfect age, but here's a rough guide:

| Age | What works well |
|---|---|
| 4 to 6 | Screen-free logic games, simple sequencing toys, ScratchJr with a parent |
| 7 to 9 | Scratch: games, animations and stories |
| 10 to 12 | More advanced Scratch, then Python or HTML and CSS |
| 13 and up | Python, JavaScript and web development, or specialising in games, apps or AI |

It's never too late, either. Teenagers who start from zero often progress very quickly because they can read, type and reason more easily.

## Step 1: Start with their interests

The single most important thing you can do is connect coding to something your child already loves.

- **Loves games?** Make a game in Scratch, or explore [making a Roblox game](/how-to-make-a-roblox-game-for-beginners/).
- **Loves drawing or art?** Try animation in Scratch or drawing with [Python turtle](/python-turtle-drawing-for-kids/).
- **Loves stories?** Build an interactive story with characters and choices.
- **Loves music?** Make a virtual piano or drum machine.
- **Loves a sport or hobby?** Build a website about it.

When a child is building something *they* care about, motivation takes care of itself.

## Step 2: Choose the right first tool

For most children, start with **block-based coding**. Blocks snap together like puzzle pieces, so there's no frustration from typos or missing brackets, and children can focus on ideas. Scratch is free, works in a web browser, and is used by millions of children worldwide.

When they're confident with blocks, usually somewhere between 10 and 12, move to a text language like Python. Our guide to the [best programming languages for kids](/best-programming-languages-for-kids/) explains the options, and [Scratch vs Python](/scratch-vs-python-which-first/) helps you decide when to switch.

## Step 3: Build a simple routine

Short, regular sessions beat occasional long ones. A routine that works for many families:

- **Two sessions a week of 30 to 60 minutes**, at a predictable time.
- **One small goal per session**, like "make the character jump" rather than "make a whole game".
- **Show and tell at the end.** Ask your child to show you what they made and explain how it works.

That last step is surprisingly powerful. Explaining their code helps children understand it more deeply, and your interest makes them proud of their work.

## Step 4: Follow a project, then remix it

A great pattern for learning:

1. **Follow a tutorial** step by step to build something complete. (Our [catch game](/how-to-make-a-catch-game-in-scratch/) and [maze game](/how-to-make-a-maze-game-in-scratch/) guides are written for exactly this.)
2. **Change it.** New characters, a different backdrop, faster speeds, extra levels.
3. **Build something new** using what they learned.

Tutorials teach skills; remixing builds confidence; original projects build real independence. When they run out of ideas, our list of [25 Scratch project ideas](/scratch-project-ideas-for-beginners/) has plenty more.

## Step 5: Help without taking over

When your child gets stuck, it's tempting to grab the mouse and fix it. Try asking questions instead:

- "What did you expect to happen? What actually happened?"
- "Can you show me which part isn't working?"
- "What's the last thing you changed?"
- "What could you try next?"

These questions teach the debugging mindset, which is one of the most valuable skills in coding. It's also fine to say "I don't know, let's find out together." Searching for an answer with your child models exactly how real programmers work.

Our guide on [how to debug code](/how-to-debug-code-tips-for-beginners/) has more strategies you can share.

## Step 6: Celebrate finished projects

Finishing is a skill. Celebrate when your child completes something, however small. Play their game. Share it with grandparents. Put a screenshot on the fridge. Children who feel proud of finished work want to make more.

## Signs your child might need more support

Learning at home works brilliantly for many children, but sometimes a little extra help makes a big difference. Consider a class, club or tutor if your child:

- gets stuck often and loses interest quickly,
- has outgrown free tutorials and wants structured progression,
- learns better with a teacher than alone,
- would benefit from live help when problems come up, which is when most beginners give up.

A good coding class should be project-based, follow a clear path from beginner to advanced, and let you see what your child has built.

## Balancing coding with screen time

Many parents worry about screen time, and that's understandable. It helps to think about the *kind* of screen time. Creating a game, solving problems and writing code is very different from passively scrolling or watching videos. Coding is active, creative and challenging.

That said, balance matters. Mix in screen-free activities that build the same thinking skills: logic puzzles, board games, building toys, and "unplugged" activities like the robot sandwich game in our [guide to algorithms](/what-is-an-algorithm-explained-for-kids/).

## Keeping it safe

- Use child-friendly platforms with moderation, like Scratch, which has community guidelines and moderators.
- Talk about **never sharing personal information** in projects, comments or usernames.
- Be careful with AI tools. Many have minimum ages, and children should understand that AI can make mistakes. Our explainer on [how AI chatbots work](/how-do-ai-chatbots-work-explained/) is a good conversation starter.
- Agree on rules for downloading software and sharing projects publicly.

## A simple four-week starter plan

**Week 1:** Create a Scratch account together (or use Scratch without an account). Make a sprite move and talk. Explore the sprite and sound libraries.

**Week 2:** Follow the [catch game tutorial](/how-to-make-a-catch-game-in-scratch/) together. Celebrate the first working game.

**Week 3:** Remix it. New sprites, sounds, a second falling object, a high score.

**Week 4:** Plan and build an original mini project from your child's own idea. Show it to the family.

By the end of a month, your child will understand loops, conditions, variables and events, and they'll have built something they're proud of.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I teach my child to code if I can't code?** Yes. Use beginner-friendly tools and step-by-step tutorials, learn alongside them, ask good questions, and celebrate progress. Your encouragement matters more than your technical knowledge.

**How much time should a child spend learning to code each week?** One to two hours a week, in two or three short sessions, is a great start. Many children happily spend more once they're building their own projects.

**Is coding good for children with no interest in maths?** Often, yes. Coding can make maths feel practical and fun, because numbers control things on screen like movement, score and speed.

**What if my child gets frustrated?** That's normal. Encourage short breaks, small goals and celebrating little wins. If frustration keeps returning, live help from a tutor can make a big difference.
