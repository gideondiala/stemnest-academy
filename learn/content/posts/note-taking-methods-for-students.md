Have you ever looked back at your notes before a test and found pages of words that make no sense? Or realised you copied everything the teacher said but can't remember any of it?

Good notes aren't about writing down as much as possible. They're about **understanding, organising and remembering**. The right method turns note-taking from copying into real learning. Here are five methods that work, and how to choose the right one.

## Why note-taking matters

Taking notes helps in two ways:

1. **Writing helps you think.** Deciding what's important and putting it in your own words makes your brain process the information, which helps you understand it.
2. **Notes are a revision tool.** Well-organised notes make revising much faster and easier later.

The key phrase is *in your own words*. Copying word for word lets information pass from your ears to your pen without going through your brain.

## Method 1: Cornell notes

The Cornell method was developed at Cornell University in the 1950s, and it's still one of the most popular methods for students because it builds revision right into your notes.

Divide your page into three sections:

```text
+----------------+-------------------------------+
|   CUE COLUMN   |          NOTES                |
|                |                               |
| Key questions  | Main ideas, facts, examples   |
| and keywords   | written during the lesson     |
|                |                               |
+----------------+-------------------------------+
|                 SUMMARY                        |
|     2 or 3 sentences in your own words         |
+------------------------------------------------+
```

**How to use it:**

1. **During the lesson:** write notes in the big right-hand section. Keep them short.
2. **After the lesson:** write questions or keywords in the left column that match your notes. For example, next to notes about photosynthesis, write "What does a plant need for photosynthesis?"
3. **At the bottom:** summarise the whole page in two or three sentences.
4. **To revise:** cover the notes section and try to answer the questions in the left column. That's active recall, one of the most effective ways to learn.

**Best for:** most subjects, especially ones with lots of facts and ideas.

## Method 2: Mind maps

A mind map starts with the main topic in the centre of the page, with branches spreading out for each big idea, and smaller branches for details.

**How to make one:**

1. Write the topic in the middle and draw a circle around it.
2. Draw thick branches for the main ideas, each in a different colour.
3. Add thinner branches for details, examples and facts.
4. Use single keywords, small drawings and symbols rather than full sentences.

**Why it works:** mind maps show how ideas connect, and colours and pictures make them more memorable.

**Best for:** revising whole topics, planning essays, and visual learners who like to see the big picture.

## Method 3: The outline method

Outlining organises notes into main points and sub-points using indentation.

```text
1. The Water Cycle
   a. Evaporation
      - Sun heats water in seas, lakes and rivers
      - Water turns into water vapour
   b. Condensation
      - Vapour cools and forms clouds
   c. Precipitation
      - Water falls as rain, snow or hail
   d. Collection
      - Water gathers in oceans, rivers and underground
```

**Why it works:** the structure makes the relationship between ideas obvious, and it's quick to write once you get used to it.

**Best for:** well-organised lessons and subjects like science, history and geography, especially when typing notes.

## Method 4: Charts and tables

When you need to compare things, a table is often the clearest way.

| | Mammals | Reptiles | Birds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Body covering | Hair or fur | Scales | Feathers |
| Body temperature | Warm-blooded | Cold-blooded | Warm-blooded |
| Young | Mostly born live | Mostly lay eggs | Lay eggs |

**Why it works:** tables make comparisons and patterns jump out, and they're great for quick revision.

**Best for:** comparing several things across the same categories, such as animals, historical events, countries or programming languages.

## Method 5: Sketch notes

Sketch notes, sometimes called visual notes, mix words with drawings, arrows, boxes and icons. You don't need to be good at art: stick figures and simple shapes are perfect.

**Why it works:** combining words and pictures gives your brain two ways to remember the same information.

**Best for:** creative learners, processes and stories, and keeping yourself engaged in longer lessons.

## Which method should you use?

| Situation | Try |
|---|---|
| Lots of facts to learn and revise | Cornell notes |
| Seeing the big picture of a topic | Mind map |
| A very organised lesson or textbook chapter | Outline |
| Comparing several things | Table |
| You like drawing or remember pictures easily | Sketch notes |

You don't have to stick to one. Many students take outline notes in class, then turn them into a mind map or Cornell questions when revising. Experiment and see what works for you.

## Tips for better notes in any method

- **Use your own words.** If you can't rephrase it, you might not understand it yet. Ask a question.
- **Keep it short.** Write keywords and phrases, not every word.
- **Use abbreviations and symbols** consistently: → for "leads to", ↑ for "increases", e.g. for "for example".
- **Leave space** so you can add things later.
- **Highlight sparingly.** If everything is highlighted, nothing stands out.
- **Review within 24 hours.** Looking over your notes the same day or the next day helps you remember much more.
- **Date and title every page** so you can find things quickly.

## Paper or screen?

Both can work. Some studies suggest that handwriting notes can help understanding, because writing by hand is slower and forces you to summarise rather than copy. Typing is faster and easier to organise and search. A good approach: write by hand in lessons when you can, and use digital tools for organising and revising.

## Notes for coding

Note-taking is just as useful when learning to code. Keep a "coding notebook" with:

- new keywords and what they do (like `for` loops or `if` statements),
- small code examples that worked,
- errors you met and how you fixed them.

That last one is gold. Next time you see the same error, you'll know exactly what to do. Our guide to [debugging your code](/how-to-debug-code-tips-for-beginners/) has more tips.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the best note-taking method for students?** There's no single best method. Cornell notes are a great all-rounder because they build in revision questions, while mind maps work well for seeing how a topic fits together.

**Should I copy exactly what the teacher writes?** Write down key facts accurately, but put explanations in your own words. That's where the learning happens.

**How do I take notes when the teacher talks fast?** Focus on keywords, use abbreviations, and leave gaps. Fill in the details from a textbook or a friend's notes afterwards.

**Is it better to rewrite my notes neatly?** Simply copying them out again isn't very useful. Turning them into something new, like a mind map, flashcards or Cornell questions, is much better.
